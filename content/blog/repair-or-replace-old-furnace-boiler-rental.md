It's December, the heating season is in full swing, and a tenant calls to say the heat is out. The technician fixes it, or quotes a repair, and then says the line every landlord eventually hears: "Honestly, this unit is getting old. You might want to think about replacing it."

Now you have to decide, often fast and often with a cold tenant waiting. This post walks through a simple way to make the repair-or-replace call on a furnace or boiler in a rental, and how to set things up so the next decision is less rushed.

## Start with three facts

Before any rule of thumb, get three pieces of information written down. Most bad decisions come from deciding without them.

1. **The age of the equipment.** Check the data plate for a manufacture date or decode the serial number. If you're not sure how, we have a separate guide on finding the age of a furnace, boiler or water heater.
2. **The repair quote, in writing.** Ask what failed, what the repair includes, and whether the technician expects other parts to fail soon.
3. **The repair history.** How many service calls in the last two or three years? A unit that has needed one repair in a decade is different from one that needs a visit every winter.

With those three facts, you can compare the system's age to its typical lifespan. You can look up typical ranges for furnaces and boilers at /lifespan. Boilers often outlast furnaces, but the range for both is wide and depends on maintenance, water quality and how hard the system works.

## Rules of thumb, and where they break

You'll hear a few common shortcuts from contractors and other owners:

- **The 50% rule.** If the repair costs more than about half of a replacement, lean toward replacing.
- **The age-times-cost rule.** Multiply the system's age by the repair cost. If the result is above a set threshold, replace. Different contractors use different thresholds, so treat it loosely.
- **The end-of-life rule.** If the unit is already past its typical lifespan, a major repair is usually money spent on borrowed time.

These are useful starting points, not answers. They break down in a few situations:

- **Cheap repair, old unit.** A small part on a very old furnace is often worth fixing to get through the winter, as long as you start planning the replacement now.
- **Expensive repair, young unit.** A big repair on a system with years of life left is usually worth doing, especially if parts or labor are under warranty.
- **Safety issues.** A cracked heat exchanger, venting problems or carbon monoxide concerns change the conversation entirely. Follow the technician's safety guidance first and run the numbers second.

## Rental-specific factors homeowners don't have to weigh

A furnace in your own house and a furnace in a rental are different decisions. A few things to factor in:

**Heat is usually a legal requirement.** Many cities and states set minimum indoor temperatures and response times for heat outages. A repair that restores heat today may matter more than the perfect long-term choice next week.

**Who pays the utilities.** If tenants pay for heat, efficiency gains mostly help them, which can still help with retention and vacancy. If you pay for heat, for example with a central boiler, a more efficient system shows up directly in your operating costs.

**Units installed at the same time.** In small multifamily buildings, furnaces or water heaters were often all installed in the same year. If one is failing from age, the others are probably close behind. That's worth knowing before you decide, because one replacement may really be the first of several.

**Timing and contractor availability.** Mid-winter replacements can mean longer waits and less room to compare quotes. If an old unit can be repaired safely now, you may get better options by planning a replacement for spring or early fall.

**Turnover.** If a unit is about to turn over, replacing equipment while it's vacant is usually simpler than working around a tenant.

## A simple decision walkthrough

When the call comes in, try working through these questions in order:

1. **Is it safe?** If the technician flags a safety issue, act on that first.
2. **Can heat be restored quickly?** If yes, that buys you time to decide calmly.
3. **Where is the unit in its lifespan?** Early, middle, or near or past the end?
4. **How does the repair compare to replacement?** Get a rough replacement quote even if you plan to repair.
5. **Is this a pattern?** Repeated calls on the same unit point toward replacement.
6. **Are other units the same age?** If so, plan for the group, not just this one.
7. **Can you fund the replacement now?** If not, a repair plus a dated plan is often the practical answer.

Write down what you decided and why. Next winter, you'll be glad you did.

## If you repair, plan the replacement anyway

Choosing to repair an aging system isn't a mistake. It's often the right call. The mistake is repairing and then forgetting about it until the next failure, usually on a cold night.

After any significant repair on an older furnace or boiler:

- **Record the date, the repair, and the cost** next to the unit's age.
- **Pick a target replacement year**, based on its age and typical lifespan.
- **Start setting money aside for it.** Divide the expected replacement cost by the months until your target date. That gives you a concrete monthly amount instead of a vague worry.
- **Repeat for the other big systems** in the building: roofs, water heaters and the rest of the HVAC.
