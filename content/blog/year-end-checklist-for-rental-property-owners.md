---

The end of the year is a natural moment to step back from day-to-day landlording and look at the bigger picture. You have a full year of repairs, replacements and receipts to learn from, and a new year to plan for.

Here's a checklist you can work through in an afternoon. It's written for owners with a handful of units up to a few dozen, but most of it applies at any size.

## 1. List what you replaced this year

Go through the year and write down every major replacement: a roof, a furnace, a water heater, appliances, flooring.

For each one, note:

- **What it was** and which property or unit.
- **When** it was replaced.
- **What it cost**, all in, including labor and disposal.

This list does two things. It updates your records with new install dates, which resets the clock on those systems. And it gives you real local costs to plan with next time, which are much better than national averages.

## 2. Update ages and conditions

For anything you didn't replace, add a year to its age. Then take a quick look at the big systems if you can:

- Any signs the roof is aging, like curling shingles or granules in the gutters?
- Did the AC struggle this summer, or the furnace this winter?
- Any water heaters showing rust or small leaks?

Condition notes help you decide which systems might come due early.

## 3. See what's due next year

With updated ages, look at which systems are at or near the end of their typical lifespan. The [component lifespan chart](/lifespan) has common ranges if you need them.

Make a short list of what's likely in the next 12 months and roughly what each would cost. This is the list that should drive your reserve planning, not a percentage of rent.

## 4. Check your reserves

Compare what you have set aside for capital expenses with what's coming due:

- **Is the balance enough** to cover next year's likely replacements?
- **Did you spend more than you saved** this year? If so, why?
- **Is a big year coming** in the next three to five years, when several systems line up?

If the numbers don't line up, now is the time to adjust your monthly set-aside. A small increase starting in January is much easier than a scramble in July. The [reserve calculator](/reserve-calculator) gives a quick estimate if you want a starting point.

## 5. Gather your records

Pull together the paperwork you'll need for taxes and for your own planning:

- Receipts and invoices for repairs and replacements.
- Rent rolls and income for each property.
- Insurance policies and any claims.
- Contracts with vendors and property managers.

Keep replacements separate from ordinary repairs. Your tax preparer will want to know which is which, and the difference matters for your own planning too.

## 6. Plan the work you can schedule

Some replacements don't have to wait for a failure. If a system is clearly near the end, replacing it on your own schedule is usually cheaper and less disruptive than an emergency call.

- **Book off-season work early.** AC work is easier to schedule in the spring, roofing in drier months, furnace work before the cold.
- **Time it with turnover.** Flooring, paint and appliances are easiest to replace between tenants.
- **Get quotes now** for anything big you expect next year.

## 7. Write down one lesson

Finish with one sentence about what you'd do differently. Maybe it's "start tracking water heater ages," "raise the monthly reserve," or "get two quotes for anything over a set amount." One lesson a year adds up.

## Keep it simple

None of this needs special software. A spreadsheet with one row per system, its age, its expected life and its replacement cost gets you most of the way. If you'd like a head start, there's a free [CapEx spreadsheet template](/capex-spreadsheet-template) you can download and fill in.
