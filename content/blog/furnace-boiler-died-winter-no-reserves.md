It's mid-December, the first real cold snap arrives, and a tenant texts at 11 p.m.: no heat. The furnace or boiler is done. You check your accounts and there's no capex reserve, or not one big enough to cover a replacement.

This is one of the most stressful moments in small-scale rental ownership. It's also very fixable if you take it in order. Here's a practical sequence: keep people safe tonight, get a sound decision on the equipment, pay for it without wrecking your finances, and set things up so the next failure doesn't land the same way.

## The first 24 hours: keep tenants warm and safe

Before you think about money, think about the people in the building. A heating failure in winter is an emergency in most places, and many local codes set minimum indoor temperatures and response times. Look up your city or county rules so you know what's required of you.

In the first day:

- **Call a licensed HVAC or boiler tech for an emergency visit.** Ask whether they can do a temporary repair to restore heat even if the unit needs replacing soon.
- **Provide temporary heat if it can't be restored quickly.** Oil-filled electric space heaters are a common stopgap. Tell tenants not to use ovens or gas stoves for heat, and remind them to keep heaters clear of bedding and curtains.
- **Protect the pipes.** In a cold building with no heat, frozen pipes can turn one problem into two. Ask tenants to keep cabinet doors under sinks open and let faucets drip if temperatures drop well below freezing.
- **Check carbon monoxide detectors.** A failing fuel-burning appliance is exactly when you want those working.
- **Communicate in writing.** A short text or email saying what happened, what you've done, and when the next update is coming goes a long way.

Keep receipts for everything, including heaters you buy. They belong in your records for this repair.

## Get a clear repair-or-replace answer

Once heat is stable, you need a real decision, not a panic one. Ask the tech to put their assessment in writing: what failed, what a repair would cost, and what a replacement would cost.

A few questions worth asking:

- How old is the unit, and what's the typical life for this type?
- Is the failed part a one-off, or a sign the heat exchanger, burner or controls are wearing out?
- If I repair now, what's likely to fail next?
- Is the current unit sized correctly for the building?

If the unit is near or past its expected life, a large repair is often money spent on equipment you'll replace soon anyway. If it's mid-life with one bad part, repair usually makes sense. Get a second quote on any replacement if your local code and the temperature allow you a few days. You can check typical lifespans for heating equipment on our [lifespan page](/lifespan).

## How to pay for it without reserves

If there's no reserve fund, you still have options. Each has trade-offs, so pick based on cost and how fast you can pay it back.

- **Cash from other accounts.** Personal savings or reserves held for another building. Simple and no interest, but write down that you borrowed from yourself so you actually pay it back.
- **Contractor or manufacturer financing.** Many HVAC companies offer installment plans. Read the terms closely, especially what happens when any promotional period ends.
- **A business line of credit or HELOC.** Usually lower rates than credit cards, but it takes time to set up. Better to open one before an emergency than during one.
- **A credit card.** Fast, and sometimes the only option at midnight. It's also typically the most expensive way to carry a balance, so treat it as a bridge.
- **Insurance.** Most policies don't cover equipment that wore out from age. They may cover damage caused by a sudden event, like frozen and burst pipes. Call your agent if anything beyond the equipment itself was damaged.

Whatever you choose, make a repayment target. For example, if a replacement came to $12,000 and you want it paid back in two years, that's $500 a month you need to find from the building's cash flow. Seeing that number written down makes the next step obvious.

## Rebuild your reserve the right way

After a surprise like this, it's tempting to just bump a flat percentage of rent and move on. A better approach is to base reserves on the actual equipment in the building.

1. **List every major system.** Roof, furnaces or boilers, water heaters, AC or heat pumps. Note the building and unit for each.
2. **Record the age of each.** Data plates, permits and old invoices usually tell you.
3. **Estimate remaining life and replacement cost.** Use local quotes where you have them.
4. **Spread the cost over the remaining years.** A water heater with three years left and an estimated cost of $1,800 needs about $600 a year set aside, or $50 a month.
5. **Add it all up and divide by units.** That gives you a monthly reserve figure for each unit you can actually defend.

Your new boiler or furnace now goes on the list at age zero, which helps. The ones that need attention are the systems that are old and not yet funded. ## Prevent the next winter surprise

A few habits make the next failure far less painful:

- **Schedule fall servicing** for every heating system, and ask the tech to note age and condition in writing.
- **Know which systems are past typical life** going into winter, and line up a contractor in advance for those.
- **Open a line of credit while you don't need it**, as a backup to cash reserves.
- **Keep a few space heaters on hand** so you can respond the same night.
- **Revisit your reserve list each year**, especially after buying another building.
