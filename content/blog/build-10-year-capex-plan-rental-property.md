The last week of December is a good time to look further ahead than next month's rent roll. The holidays slow things down, tenants are mostly settled, and January is when a lot of owners set their numbers for the year.

If you only do one planning exercise this week, make it a 10-year capital expense plan. It's a simple list of the big things in your buildings that will wear out, when they're likely to go, and roughly what they'll cost. Once you have it, the question "how much should I set aside?" gets a real answer instead of a guess.

Here's how to build one, step by step, with nothing fancier than a spreadsheet.

## What a 10-year capex plan is (and isn't)

A capex plan covers the expensive, predictable replacements: roofs, furnaces and boilers, AC condensers, water heaters, and anything else you'd consider a major system. It is not your repair budget. A leaky faucet or a service call stays in operating expenses.

Ten years is a useful window because it's long enough to catch most of the big items coming up, but short enough that your estimates stay grounded. A roof that's 22 years old shows up. A roof installed last spring mostly doesn't, though it still belongs on the list.

It also isn't a professional reserve study. It's a working plan you own and update. For a building with a handful of units, that's usually what you need.

## Build your system inventory

Start with one row per system, per building. For each, write down:

- **Building and unit** (or "common" for shared systems like a boiler or roof)
- **System type** (roof, boiler, furnace, condenser, water heater)
- **Install year**, or your best estimate
- **Brand and model**, if you have it

The install year is the column most owners are missing. Check closing documents, old invoices, permit records, and the data plate on the equipment itself. Many water heaters and furnaces have the manufacture date encoded in the serial number. If you truly can't find it, write an estimate and mark it as one so you know to confirm it later.

If you'd rather not set up columns from scratch, the [capex spreadsheet template](/capex-spreadsheet-template) has this layout ready to fill in.

## Estimate when each item is due and what it costs

For each row, add two more columns: **expected replacement year** and **estimated cost**.

For the replacement year, take the install year and add a typical lifespan for that system. The [lifespan reference](/lifespan) lists common ranges for roofs, HVAC and water heaters. Then adjust for what you know: a water heater in an area with hard water, or a furnace that's been nursed along with repairs, probably sits on the short end.

For cost, use your own numbers wherever possible. A recent quote, an invoice from a similar job, or a call to the contractor you already use will beat any national average. Costs vary a lot by region and building, so a rough local figure is more useful than a precise one from somewhere else.

Anything already past its expected life goes in year 1. That's not a prediction it fails in January, just a signal that you should be ready if it does.

## Lay it out year by year

Now sort your list by replacement year and total up each year. This is where the plan starts paying for itself, because you'll see the lumpy years.

Here's a made-up example for an 8-unit building:

- **Boiler**, due in year 3: $15,000
- **Roof**, due in year 7: $40,000
- **8 water heaters**, spread across years 1 through 9: $1,800 each, $14,400 total

Total over 10 years: $69,400. But it doesn't arrive evenly. Years 3 and 7 are heavy, and several others are light.

Seeing that on one page tells you a lot. You might decide to replace two water heaters early, in a quiet year, so they don't land in the same year as the roof. Or you might realize the boiler in year 3 needs attention now, while you still have time to save.

## Turn it into a monthly reserve per unit

The simplest version: divide the 10-year total by 120 months, then by the number of units.

In the example, $69,400 divided by 120 is about $578 a month for the building, or about $72 a unit a month.

Then do one check that a lot of owners skip: make sure the cash is there in time. A flat monthly number works on average, but a big item early in the plan can arrive before you've saved enough.

In the example, by the end of year 3 you'd have saved about $20,800. If three water heaters plus the boiler come due by then, that's $20,400. It works, but barely. If the boiler were due in year 2, you'd come up short. In that case, set aside more in the early years, or count any reserves you already hold toward the early items.

If you already have money set aside, subtract it from the total before dividing. The [reserve calculator](/reserve-calculator) does this math, and the [capex calculator](/capex-calculator) helps if you want to start from a single building.

This is also a good way to sanity-check a flat rule like 5 to 10 percent of rent. Sometimes it lines up with your plan. Sometimes an older building needs a lot more, and a newer one needs less.

## Keep it current

A capex plan only helps if it reflects your actual buildings. A few habits keep it accurate:

- **Update it when something gets replaced.** Change the install year and reset the next replacement date.
- **Add new buildings the same way.** One row per system, same columns, so the plan doesn't turn into a mess of separate tabs.
- **Review it once a year.** Late December or early January works well. Refresh costs, confirm estimated ages, and recalculate the monthly number.

Either way, an hour or two this week gives you a clear number to carry into the new year, and fewer surprises when the cold hits hardest.
