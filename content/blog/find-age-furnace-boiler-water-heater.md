December is when heating equipment does its hardest work. Furnaces and boilers run for long stretches, and water heaters take in colder water and work harder to warm it. If one of them is near the end of its life, winter is a likely time for it to fail.

You can't plan for a replacement if you don't know how old the equipment is. Many small landlords inherited their systems from a previous owner and never checked. The good news is that age is usually findable in a few minutes per unit, without a technician.

Here is how to do it, and what to write down once you know.

## Start with the data plate

Every furnace, boiler, water heater and outdoor AC unit has a rating plate or sticker from the manufacturer. That is your best source.

Where to find it:

- **Gas or electric water heaters:** usually a large sticker on the side of the tank, often near the gas valve or the upper element cover.
- **Furnaces:** inside the front panel, on the blower compartment door or the cabinet wall. Turn off power at the service switch before removing a panel.
- **Boilers:** on the side or front of the jacket, sometimes behind a removable cover.
- **Outdoor AC or heat pump units:** on the side of the cabinet, often near the electrical connections.

Take a clear photo of the whole plate before you try to read it. A phone flashlight held at an angle helps with faded print. The photo also gives you a permanent record, so you don't have to go back into the unit later.

Some plates list a manufacture date in plain words. If yours does, you're done. If not, the serial number is the next step.

## How to read the serial number

Most manufacturers encode the month and year of manufacture inside the serial number. The trouble is that each brand does it differently, and some brands have changed their format over the years.

Common patterns you'll see:

- **Four digits for week or month and year**, such as a section that reads like a two-digit month followed by a two-digit year.
- **A letter for the month**, where A stands for January, B for February, and so on, paired with digits for the year.
- **Letter codes on a repeating cycle**, where the same letter can mean years that are decades apart. For these you need the brand's own chart and some common sense about which decade fits.

The reliable way to decode it:

1. Write down the brand, model number and full serial number from your photo.
2. Search for the brand name plus "serial number date code." Many manufacturers publish their own decoder or chart.
3. If you can't find an official chart, call the manufacturer's customer support line with the model and serial. They can usually tell you the build date.
4. Sanity check the result. A water heater that decodes to a date before the building's last major renovation might be original, or it might mean you read the code wrong.

Keep in mind that manufacture date and install date are not the same. Equipment can sit in a warehouse for a while before it goes in. For planning, the manufacture date is close enough and errs slightly on the cautious side.

## When the label is missing or unreadable

Older equipment often has a peeled, rusted or painted-over plate. You still have options:

- **Installer stickers.** Plumbers and HVAC contractors often put their own sticker on the unit with an install date or service history.
- **Permit records.** Many cities and counties require permits for water heater and furnace replacements. Your local building department may be able to look up permits by address.
- **Closing documents.** Your purchase inspection report frequently notes the approximate age of major systems. Check your files from when you bought the building.
- **Previous owner or property manager.** A quick email can turn up old invoices.
- **Refrigerant type on AC units.** Outdoor units built to use R-22 refrigerant are generally from before the 2010 phase-out of R-22 in new equipment. That doesn't give you an exact year, but it tells you the unit is old.
- **Your next service visit.** If a technician is already coming out, ask them to estimate the age and note it on the invoice.

If all else fails, make a reasonable estimate and label it as an estimate. A rough age you can plan around is far more useful than a blank cell.

## What to record for each system

Once you have ages, put them somewhere you'll actually look again. For each piece of equipment, record:

- Building and unit
- System type (water heater, furnace, boiler, AC, heat pump)
- Brand, model and serial number
- Fuel type and size (for example, a 40-gallon gas tank)
- Manufacture or install year, and whether it's confirmed or estimated
- Source of the date (plate, permit, invoice, inspection report)
- Last service date and any known issues
- Photo of the data plate

## Turn ages into a replacement plan

Age on its own is just a number. It becomes useful when you compare it against a typical lifespan for that type of equipment and see which items are coming due.

A simple way to sort what you found:

1. **Past typical lifespan:** expect replacement soon. Get a quote now so you aren't shopping in an emergency.
2. **Within a few years of typical lifespan:** start setting money aside specifically for these.
3. **Mid-life or newer:** keep up with routine maintenance and check again next year.

Water heaters in multiple units often share a similar age if they were installed during the same renovation. That can mean several replacements land close together, which is worth knowing before it happens.
