The end of December is when a lot of small landlords open their spreadsheet, scroll through a year of receipts, and wonder which ones were "real" capital expenses and which were just repairs. A $400 water heater element and a $1,600 water heater replacement both say "water heater" on the invoice. They mean very different things for your reserves.

This post walks through a simple way to sort rental costs into repairs, maintenance and capital expenses. The goal is to make your reserve planning honest and your records easy to hand to whoever does your taxes.

One note up front. Tax rules for repairs versus improvements are detailed, and they change. This post is about planning and record-keeping. For how to treat a specific expense on your return, ask your tax preparer.

## Why the line matters for your reserves

Your capex reserve exists to pay for big, predictable replacements: roofs, furnaces, boilers, water heaters, AC condensers. If repairs keep getting paid out of that fund, two things happen.

- The reserve drains faster than your plan expected, so the next real replacement catches you short.
- Your records make it look like your systems cost more to own than they do, which skews next year's reserve number.

The reverse is also a problem. If you log a full boiler replacement as a "repair," you lose the install date. That date is the single most useful piece of information for forecasting when the next one is due.

So the sorting isn't busywork. It keeps your reserve fund pointed at the right job and keeps your system ages accurate.

## A simple test: repair, maintenance or capital expense?

For planning purposes, three questions get you most of the way there.

1. **Did it replace a whole system or major component?** A new roof, a new furnace, a new water heater, a new compressor. If yes, treat it as a capital expense.
2. **Did it make the system last meaningfully longer than it would have otherwise?** Re-roofing one full slope, re-tubing a boiler, replacing a heat exchanger. These usually belong in capex too.
3. **Did it just keep things working the way they already were?** Fixing a leak, swapping a thermostat, replacing a pilot assembly. That's a repair.

Maintenance is the routine stuff you do on a schedule whether anything is broken or not: filter changes, boiler cleanings, flushing water heaters, gutter cleaning. It belongs in your operating budget, not your reserve.

A quick rule of thumb: if the work resets the clock on a system's lifespan, it's capex. If the clock keeps ticking where it was, it's a repair or maintenance.

## Examples from a typical small building

Here's how common winter and year-round costs usually sort out for planning.

**Usually capital expenses**
- Full roof tear-off and replacement
- New furnace, boiler or heat pump
- New water heater (tank or tankless)
- Replacing an AC condenser or full split system
- Replacing all windows in a unit

**Usually repairs**
- Patching a roof leak or replacing a few shingles after a storm
- Replacing an igniter, flame sensor, circulator pump or thermostat
- Replacing a water heater thermostat, element or anode rod
- Clearing a frozen or burst pipe and patching drywall

**Usually maintenance**
- Annual furnace or boiler service
- Filter changes
- Water heater flushing
- Gutter and downspout cleaning

These aren't tax categories, just a planning sort. Your preparer may classify some items differently, and that's fine. You can keep a column for their treatment separately.

## The gray areas

A few situations trip people up.

**Partial replacements.** You replace one slope of a roof, or the compressor but not the whole AC system. For planning, log it as capex and note what was replaced. Don't reset the age of the whole system. A roof with one new slope still has three old ones.

**Repeated repairs on an old system.** Three repair calls on a 22-year-old boiler in one winter are each repairs. But together they're a signal that a capital expense is close. Flag the system in your log so it moves up in your forecast.

**Emergency replacements.** A furnace that dies on a January night and gets replaced the next morning is still a capital expense, even though it felt like an emergency repair. Record it with the install date, model and cost.

**Upgrades.** Swapping a standard tank water heater for a tankless unit is a capital expense. It may also change the expected lifespan, so update that in your plan.

## How to set up your log for the new year

January is a good time to start clean. A simple log needs only a few columns:

- Date
- Property and unit
- System (roof, furnace, boiler, water heater, AC)
- Type: repair, maintenance or capex
- What was done, in one line
- Cost
- Paid from: operating account or reserve
- New install date, if it was a replacement

The "paid from" column is the one most people skip, and it's the one that tells you whether your reserve is doing its job. If repairs keep landing in the reserve, either move them back to operating or raise your operating budget.

The "new install date" column is what keeps your system ages current. Every time you log a capital expense, update the age of that system in your forecast. If you don't have a sheet set up yet, our free [capex spreadsheet template](/capex-spreadsheet-template) has these columns ready to go.
